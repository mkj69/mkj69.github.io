点估计：估计值固定以后，估计器仍有抽样分布

5.4.1 在页 120 至 122 区分真实参数 θ 与估计器 θ̂ₘ。频率学派把 θ 视为固定但未知，训练集 D 则随机。给定一次已观察的 D，计算出的值确定；在可能的训练集之间，g(D) 是随机变量。估计器的定义本身不保证准确，也不保证输出落在合法参数集合中。

偏差：平均方向正确不等于每次估计准确

5.4.2 在页 122 定义偏差为重复抽样期望与真实参数之差。无偏是关于估计程序的性质，并非已经观察到的误差等于零。渐近无偏只要求偏差随 m 趋于零，也没有直接限制估计器的离散程度。

估计总体方差：为什么分母 m 会少算一份自由度

页 124 至 125 比较两个方差估计器。先用相同数据拟合均值，再计算残差，会把残差平方和压低。分母为 m 的估计量平均低估 σ²；分母改为 m−1 后得到无偏估计。这里 m>1，样本独立同分布且二阶矩有限。

标准误衡量估计量变化，不是单个样本的标准差

5.4.3 的方差 Var_D(θ̂ₘ) 与标准误 SE(θ̂ₘ) 描述重新抽取整个数据集时估计会怎样变化。对于独立同分布样本，均值方差为 σ²/m，标准误为 σ/√m。数据本身的标准差仍是 σ。样本数扩大 4 倍，均值标准误缩小为原来的一半。这个比例是公式推论，不是新增实验结果。

SE(θ̂ₘ)=√Var_D(θ̂ₘ); Var(μ̂ₘ)=σ²/m; SE(μ̂ₘ)=σ/√m. For Bernoulli data: Var(θ̂ₘ)=θ(1−θ)/m.

原书公式 5.45 至 5.46 及 5.48 至 5.52，印刷页 125 至 127。

95% 区间：重复抽样覆盖率与比较方法

页 126 使用中心极限定理给出近似正态区间，系数为 1.96。频率学派的 95% 指重复生成数据并构造区间时，约 95% 的区间包含固定真值。已计算的某个区间是否包含真值已经确定，不能直接把该覆盖率解释为后验概率。此处通常采用独立同分布、有限非零方差且样本量足够大的近似；小样本或重尾损失未必可靠。

MSE 分解：有偏估计可以有更小的总误差

5.4.4 在页 127 用参数估计的均方误差同时衡量偏差与方差。以下分解假设估计器有有限二阶矩。令 a=E_D[θ̂ₘ]，把误差写成 (θ̂ₘ−a)+(a−θ)。平方后交叉项的期望为零，因为 E_D[θ̂ₘ−a]=0。这解释了原书 5.53 至 5.54，而不只是记住两项相加。

MSE(θ̂ₘ)=E_D[(θ̂ₘ−θ)²]=Var_D(θ̂ₘ)+(E_D[θ̂ₘ]−θ)².

原书公式 5.53 至 5.54，印刷页 127。

此处参数 MSE 不含额外观测噪声。笔记补充的预测版本需要独立新观测 y=f(x)+ε、E[ε|x]=0，且条件噪声与训练集独立。在固定 x 上，预测 MSE 等于模型偏差平方、训练集造成的预测方差与不可约噪声方差之和。跨不同 x 的风险还要再对输入分布取平均。

E_D,ε[(f̂_D(x)−y)²|x]=(E_D[f̂_D(x)]−f(x))²+Var_D(f̂_D(x))+Var(ε|x).

笔记原创推导，扩展原书公式 5.53 至 5.54；噪声条件在相邻正文中注明。

原书在页 127 把交叉验证作为实际中常用的偏差方差权衡方法。参数 MSE 的定义依赖未知 θ 和重复抽样期望，单个数据集通常不能直接给出。笔记补充：交叉验证在未参与当前拟合的样本上估计预测损失，帮助比较学习程序；它不直接分别测量参数偏差与方差，预测损失也未必等同于参数 MSE。

相合性：数据增加时，偏离真值的概率趋于零

5.4.5 在页 128 至 129 定义弱相合性为依概率收敛。任意固定误差容忍度 ε>0 下，离真值超过 ε 的概率随 m 趋于零。强相合性要求几乎必然收敛。两者都描述一列估计程序，不是某一次训练集的经验分数。

θ̂ₘ →ₚ θ ⇔ for every ε>0, P(|θ̂ₘ−θ|>ε)→0. Strong consistency: P(limₘ→∞ θ̂ₘ=θ)=1.

原书公式 5.55 及相邻弱相合性、强相合性定义，印刷页 128 至 129。

Section 5.4.2, p. 122, defines bias as the difference between the sampling expectation and the true parameter. Unbiasedness describes the estimation procedure. It does not say that an observed estimate has zero error. Asymptotic unbiasedness only requires bias to vanish with m. It does not directly restrict dispersion.

bias(θ̂ₘ)=E_D[θ̂ₘ]−θ; unbiased: E_D[θ̂ₘ]=θ; asymptotically unbiased: limₘ→∞ E_D[θ̂ₘ]=θ.

The MSE decomposition permits a biased estimator to win

Section 5.4.4, p. 127, uses parameter MSE to combine bias and variance. The decomposition below assumes a finite estimator second moment. With a=E_D[θ̂ₘ], the error becomes (θ̂ₘ−a)+(a−θ). The cross term has zero expectation because E_D[θ̂ₘ−a]=0. This derives Equations 5.53 to 5.54.

MSE(θ̂ₘ)=E_D[(θ̂ₘ−θ)²]=Var_D(θ̂ₘ)+(E_D[θ̂ₘ]−θ)².

This parameter MSE has no extra observation noise term. The prediction version below is note analysis. Consistency makes the probability of a fixed estimation error vanish

Section 5.4.5, pp. 128 to 129, defines weak consistency as convergence in probability. For every fixed tolerance ε>0, the probability of an error larger than ε tends to zero as m grows. Strong consistency requires almost sure convergence. Both describe a sequence of procedures, rather than the empirical score from one dataset.

θ̂ₘ →ₚ θ ⇔ for every ε>0, P(|θ̂ₘ−θ|>ε)→0. Strong consistency: P(limₘ→∞ θ̂ₘ=θ)=1.

Source Eq. 5.55 and adjacent weak and strong consistency definitions, printed pp. 128 to 129.

The book’s counterexample estimates a Gaussian mean using only the first sample. It is unbiased for every m, but its variance remains σ². More data never change the estimate. The sample mean instead has variance σ²/m. Chebyshev’s inequality then proves consistency. This added proof assumes i.i.d. samples and finite variance.

A technical qualification: consistency alone does not imply convergence of expectations

Page 129 states that consistency makes bias vanish. This is reasonable for many common estimators with controlled moments, but it is not an unconditional theorem. Convergence in probability controls the probability of large errors. Rare outputs with very large magnitudes can still dominate the expectation. Convergence of expectations needs an additional condition such as uniform integrability.

The following mathematical counterexample is note analysis. A data variable U has distribution Uniform(0,1), the target is 0, and Tₘ=m·1{U≤1/m}. Then Tₘ converges in probability to 0 while its expectation stays at 1. This is a logical counterexample, not a proposed learning algorithm. Uniform boundedness excludes this tail problem, as it does for a Bernoulli sample mean in [0,1].

Maximum likelihood compares parameters with the data held fixed

Section 5.5, pp. 129 to 130, chooses parameters that make the observed data most likely. A likelihood holds X fixed and varies θ. It is not a probability distribution over θ. KL divergence, cross entropy and NLL share an optimum under stated conditions

For discrete distributions with compatible support, the decomposition on p. 130 is direct. The empirical distribution is fixed, so E log p̂_data does not vary with θ. Minimizing KL therefore minimizes average NLL. Cross entropy is not specific to Bernoulli or softmax models. It is defined relative to distributions and a logarithmic score. Assigning zero probability to an observed event gives infinite NLL.

D_KL(p̂_data∥p_θ)=E_p̂[log p̂_data]−E_p̂[log p_θ]; H(p̂_data,p_θ)=−E_p̂[log p_θ]=H(p̂_data)+D_KL(p̂_data∥p_θ).

Source Eqs. 5.60 to 5.61, printed p. 130; the entropy identity is note notation for the discrete case.

Continuous data need a qualification. An atomic empirical measure is generally singular relative to a continuous model measure. Its literal KL is not a finite quantity available for this subtraction. The book’s expression is a common formal interpretation in that setting. Evaluating log p_θ(x) at observed points and averaging is still well defined. For population densities with a common base measure and finite expectations, the population cross entropy and KL identity also holds.

A continuous density can exceed 1, so NLL can be negative, as the book notes. For example, a note calculation gives density about 3.989 for N(0,0.01) at x=0, with single point NLL about −1.384. Interval probabilities still lie in [0,1] because they integrate the density. Density values and their NLL depend on measurement units. KL remains invariant under the same invertible change of variables for both distributions.

Asymptotic efficiency needs regularity and does not settle finite sample risk

Pages 132 to 133 motivate MLE through statistical efficiency. The note restricts this claim to regular, identifiable, correctly specified finite dimensional models. With nonsingular per observation Fisher information I(θ) and suitable smoothness and moment conditions, the MLE is often asymptotically normal with covariance proportional to 1/m. This describes a limit rather than an exact distribution at every sample size.

√m(θ̂_ML−θ) ⇒ N(0,I(θ)⁻¹); Cov(θ̂_ML)≈I(θ)⁻¹/m. I(θ)=E_θ[(∇_θ log p_θ(x))(∇_θ log p_θ(x))ᵀ].

The book summarizes the Cramér Rao argument as excluding a lower MSE for any consistent estimator. That statement needs qualification. The classical finite sample bound first concerns unbiased estimators under its assumptions. Asymptotic efficiency comparisons also restrict the estimator class, for example to regular estimators. Consistency alone does not exclude constructions that are superefficient at special parameter values. MLE is therefore not universally optimal across all models, parameter points and losses.

The book then returns to finite data. Regularization can accept bias to reduce variance. An asymptotic result cannot decide whether that trade is useful at a particular m. The answer depends on the loss, sampling conditions, prior assumptions and validation. The source provides no sample size threshold or performance table here.

MAP turns the prior into a penalty with explicit signs and scaling

Section 5.6.1, pp. 136 to 137, selects a point of maximal posterior density. With D fixed, the evidence does not depend on θ. Maximizing log p(θ|D) therefore maximizes log p(D|θ)+log p(θ). In a minimization objective, the penalty is −log p(θ), with the opposite sign. MAP remains a point estimate and does not integrate the posterior.

With fixed noise σ² and a zero mean Gaussian prior covariance α⁻¹I, negative log posterior contains SSE/(2σ²)+α||w||²/2. Multiplication by 2σ² gives SSE+ασ²||w||². For continuous parameters, MAP maximizes a density. An individual point usually has zero probability.
